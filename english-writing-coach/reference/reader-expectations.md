# Reader Expectations (Gopen & Swan)

Source: George D. Gopen and Judith A. Swan, "The Science of Scientific Writing", *American Scientist* 78 (1990) 550–558. Synthesized, not copied; quotations are short and marked. Williams's *Style* (Rubric A) descends from the same methodology, so this file is the rationale behind Rubric A, not a second rubric. Load it when the user asks *why* a Rubric A item holds, when running a W3 argument about one, or when drilling a recurring A-item weakness as a conceptual gap.

## The premise

Readers interpret, they do not merely decode. Most of their interpretive decisions about *substance* come from clues in *structure*: readers have fixed expectations about where in a unit of discourse each kind of information will appear, and they read whatever sits in a slot as the kind of thing that slot usually holds. A unit of discourse is anything with a beginning and an end: clause, sentence, paragraph, section, article.

Consequences the coach uses directly:

- **Meaning is a probability over readers.** No sentence can be made to mean exactly one thing; structure raises the odds that most readers extract the intended meaning. So when the coach reads a sentence differently from the author, that divergence is evidence about the sentence, and "it's clear to me" is not a defense. Report what you read; let the gap speak.
- **Structure spent is content lost.** Energy a reader spends unravelling structure is unavailable for content. Moderate increases in content complexity produce disproportionate increases in misreading when structure is also unreliable.
- **Writing improves thinking.** Structural revision regularly uncovers gaps in the argument that the original structure hid. Of the article's four examples, only one could be fixed by restructuring alone; the other three needed new sentences or deletions once the structure stopped concealing the missing connections. "In real and important ways, the structure of the prose becomes the structure of the scientific argument."

## The seven principles (the authors' own list)

1. Follow a grammatical subject as soon as possible with its verb.
2. Place in the stress position the new information you want the reader to emphasize.
3. Place the person or thing whose story a sentence is telling at the beginning, in the topic position.
4. Place appropriate old information (material already stated in the discourse) in the topic position, for linkage backward and contextualization forward.
5. Articulate the action of every clause or sentence in its verb.
6. In general, provide context for your reader before asking that reader to consider anything new.
7. In general, try to ensure that the relative emphases of the substance coincide with the relative expectations for emphasis raised by the structure.

The authors insist these are principles, not rules. Every one can be violated to good effect, but the violation reads as deliberate only against a background of fulfilment. Writers who violate them habitually are not stylists; they are producing noise the reader must filter.

Rubric A mapping: 1→A6, 2→A4, 3→A1, 4→A3, 5→A2, 6→A3/F1, 7→A4/A5.

## Subject–verb separation (A6)

Readers need syntactic resolution and get it only from the verb. Until the verb arrives they hold the subject in suspense and treat everything intervening as an interruption, hence as less important. The longer the interruption, the more likely it actually contains something important, and the more surely its position marks it as not. The reader discovers the mistake only when the sentence ends having delivered nothing of value outside the interruption.

Two fixes, and the coach cannot choose between them because the choice depends on what the author meant:

- the interrupting material matters → give it its own clause and its own stress position (often via a semicolon);
- it does not → delete it and let the sentence drive to its point.

Flag the separation and the two options; the author decides. Sentence length is not the diagnosis: 100-word sentences can read easily and 10-word sentences can be impenetrable.

## The stress position (A4)

Readers emphasize what arrives at the end of a sentence, at the moment of **syntactic closure**: the point at which they know nothing remains but what they are reading. The authors' image is a mental breath drawn at the start of a sentence and exhaled at its end; the exhalation is the emphasis.

Properties:

- **Variable size.** One word, or a whole numbered list if the list has been announced as all that remains. Each list item then carries its own stress position.
- **Secondary stress positions.** A correctly used colon or semicolon creates one, because the material before it must be able to stand alone as a sentence. This is how a sentence extends to dozens of words without strain: one medial closure per piece of stress-worthy new information.
- **Two failure modes.** (a) The stress position holds material not worth stressing; the reader, with no secondary clue, guesses what else mattered. (b) The stress position holds material the writer never meant to stress; the reader stresses it anyway and the writer has lost control of the interpretation. The second is worse.
- **"Too long", defined.** A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress than it has stress positions. This replaces readability-formula word limits. Fixes: split; add a medial closure; demote or delete a candidate.
- **Flag words.** A word such as *both* or *two effects* announces how many pieces of new information will arrive in one stress position, so the reader can hold them without re-parsing.

## The topic position (A1, A3, A5)

The sentence opening supplies perspective. Two expectations attach to it:

- **Whose story.** Readers take a unit of discourse to be about whoever shows up first. "Bees disperse pollen" and "Pollen is dispersed by bees" report the same fact and are both respectable; the first is about bees, the second about pollen. In a paragraph that is pollen's story, the passive sentence is the better one. Voice follows topic, never a blanket rule.
- **Linkage and context.** The topic position should hold *old information*: material the reader has already met in this discourse. It links backward to the previous sentence and sets the frame for what follows. New information in the topic position misleads the reader about whose story is told, forces them to carry unconnected material further into the sentence, and leaves ambiguous what to emphasize.

The precise principle, which the authors warn is routinely over-simplified: *put in the topic position the old information that links backward; put in the stress position the new information you want emphasized.* Not "old in topic, new in stress": since every piece of information is one or the other, the middle of the sentence necessarily carries both.

Why writers get this wrong: they compose linearly under the anxiety of capturing the new thought before it escapes, so the new idea goes down first and the connective tissue is appended later, if at all. The result serves the writer's need to unburden, not the reader's need to receive. The authors call misplaced old/new "the No. 1 problem in American professional writing". Shitty-first-draft mode legitimately writes this way; revision reverses it.

## The three diagnostic columns

For a paragraph that reads as intelligent sentences adding up to nothing, list vertically:

1. **Topic positions** (what opens each sentence). Mostly new material, or several unrelated strands, means the paragraph has no story or is telling several at once. Find the strand of old information that keeps recurring mid-sentence; it is usually the story that should have been in the topic slots.
2. **Main verbs.** A column of *is / has / are presumed to be* means the actions are not in the verbs. Then either they are buried in nominalizations (Rubric A2 fixes this) or, worse, they are absent: the verbs that connect the players never appear anywhere, and the hypotheses the paragraph exists to state are not stated. That is a substance finding.
3. **Stress positions** (what closes each sentence). Compare against the list of what the author believes is new and emphasis-worthy. Mismatch = principle 7 violated.

## Perceiving logical gaps (A3 gap → E5)

When a sentence contains no old information at all, in the topic position or anywhere else, the reader has to invent the link to the preceding discourse. This happens when a connection is so clear in the writer's mind that stating it feels redundant. Placing old and new information deliberately exposes exactly these spots: the revision stalls because there is nothing old to put in the topic slot, and the only way forward is to write the missing sentence or cut the orphan. The coach flags `[A3 gap]`, and the author, who alone knows what the connection is, supplies it; this is the one case where the structural rubric hands work to the argument rubric (E5, warrant) rather than to sentence revision.

## Style as habit (Recurring-Weakness Loop)

"A writer's personal style is the sum of all the structural choices that person tends to make." The choices are consistent across documents, which is why one draft's pattern predicts the next's, and why the pattern is reversible: a habitual decision can be permanently changed once it is named and drilled. This is the warrant for the weakness log and for drilling a repeated item rather than re-flagging it.

## What the article does not do

It does not reduce jargon or simplify the science; the authors' revisions keep every technical term and are not shorter than the originals. Clarification, not simplification. Do not let an A-item flag turn into a request to dumb the content down.
